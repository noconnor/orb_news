> **Historical Parallel: Strategic Bombing in World War II — The Blitz and German Terror Bombing (1940–1941)**
> Nazi Germany's sustained aerial campaign against British cities from September 1940 through May 1941 sought to break civilian morale and force Britain's capitulation through industrial and residential devastation. The Luftwaffe dropped tens of thousands of tonnes of high explosives and incendiary bombs on London, Coventry, Liverpool, and other cities, killing over 40,000 civilians and rendering hundreds of thousands homeless. Britain's air defences, while improving, could not prevent all raids — the critical challenge was sustaining public resilience alongside active interception. As with the Korean War stalemate that followed a decade later, the campaign demonstrated that no amount of aerial punishment alone was sufficient to compel a determined adversary to surrender, so long as outside support — in Britain's case, American Lend-Lease, and in Ukraine's case, Western military and financial aid — remained intact. Russia's mass drone-and-missile strategy echoes this historical logic: attrition and exhaustion, not decisive battlefield victory, as the path to political capitulation.

### Pentagon Moves to Accelerate US Military Drawdown from Europe, Alarming NATO Allies

<!-- audio:top-story-3 -->
Senior Pentagon officials announced plans on Monday to reduce American military commitment in Europe faster than previously communicated, catching European allies off guard with both the pace and the scope of the proposed changes.<sup>[1](https://eu.news-pravda.com/world/2026/06/01/202609.html)</sup> The specific reductions — to troop deployments, logistical support, and military infrastructure — will be formally presented to NATO partners, but the manner of the announcement, before allies had been fully briefed, has itself become a flashpoint for transatlantic tension. Coming on the same day Russia was launching its largest overnight bombardment of Ukraine in months, the timing generated immediate alarm among European defence ministers who have spent two years urging allies to increase rather than decrease their forward posture.

The announcement accelerates a debate that has defined NATO politics since Trump's return to the White House: how much of the European security burden must Europe now absorb independently, and on what timeline? Several European governments have already begun accelerating national defence spending commitments in anticipation of reduced American guarantees, but defence industrial capacity cannot be rebuilt in months. The Pentagon's move also carries implications for the credibility of Article 5 deterrence in the Baltic states and Poland, where the US forward presence has been a cornerstone of reassurance against Russian aggression. NATO's Secretary-General is expected to respond formally later this week, with emergency consultations likely before the alliance's next scheduled summit.

> **Historical Parallel: The Post-Cold War US Army Europe Drawdown (1990–2013)**
> When the Berlin Wall fell in November 1989 and the Soviet Union dissolved two years later, the United States embarked on a sweeping reduction of its forces in Europe that offers a direct structural precedent for today's Pentagon announcement. US Army Europe contracted from 213,000 soldiers in 1990 to 122,000 in 1992 — shedding roughly 70,000 personnel and 90,000 family members in a single year as the strategic rationale of containing Soviet ground power evaporated. Physical infrastructure shrank in parallel, with installations declining from 858 in 1990 to 415 by 1993. The reductions continued into the 2000s: the 1st Armored Division relocated from Germany to Fort Bliss, Texas, the 1st Infantry Division moved to Fort Riley, Kansas, and V Corps — a command with nearly a century of European history — was formally inactivated in June 2013. That earlier drawdown was driven by the genuine elimination of the Soviet threat; the current one is being proposed while Russia is actively waging war on a European neighbour, making the strategic logic considerably more contested. The parallels also highlight a consistent American instinct — visible under Eisenhower, Nixon, and now Trump — to treat forward deployments in Europe as a temporary expedient rather than a permanent commitment, forcing European allies to periodically confront their own defence dependency.
