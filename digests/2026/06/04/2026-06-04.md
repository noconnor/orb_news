### Israel and Lebanon Agree Ceasefire as Iranian Drones Strike Kuwait

<!-- audio:top-story-1 -->
Israel and Lebanon announced the implementation of a ceasefire agreement on 4 June, with both governments declaring they reject "any attempt, by any state or non-state actor, to hold Lebanon's future hostage."<sup>[1](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c5y01pdqvkgo)</sup> The agreement was brokered through US-led diplomatic efforts after weeks of escalating Israeli military operations, including what Israeli commanders described as the deepest ground offensive into Lebanese territory in two decades.<sup>[2](http://www.euronews.com/2026/06/04/israel-and-lebanon-agree-to-conditional-ceasefire-following-us-led-talks)</sup> The ceasefire's announcement was welcomed internationally as a significant de-escalation, though its durability was immediately tested by events elsewhere in the region.

Within hours of the ceasefire taking effect, Iranian drones struck Kuwait's international airport in an attack that killed at least one person — a direct act of military aggression in the Gulf that threw the stability of the agreement into sharp relief.<sup>[3](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMitgFBVV95cUxPMWw3aG9uNmppOHRXVF9yQ2tkOGdlN3g5c2FnQllkOUxxYXhqSUVoUlRYc21pVHlHdjNFOE1nbDZjNlYta2dsVW4tUV9kekZFM2NVWUZ2UGRXSzZkXzBmSm54VTdlb2FBQkdsVHJnQ0R1aEJncGF5N2xPaEdGVDdWZG1vaS1WQU9EeVJZZWJzQlgyaWV1S21MNmhQT1pXb0tNbXBrbzQ0bmRaQXFjQ3JETEpCVVM3Zw?oc=5)</sup> Al Jazeera reported tensions continuing to simmer across the broader conflict zone as multiple state and non-state actors maintained active military postures despite diplomatic progress.<sup>[4](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/liveblog/2026/6/4/iran-war-live-tensions-simmer-after-latest-us-iran-clashes-in-gulf)</sup> The episode underscores how a bilateral ceasefire between Israel and Lebanon cannot in itself contain a regional confrontation that now involves Iran, the United States, and Gulf states as active or threatened parties.

> **Historical Parallel: The Taif Agreement — End of the Lebanese Civil War** (1989)
> Lebanon's modern political landscape has repeatedly been reshaped by externally brokered agreements that silence fighting without resolving underlying tensions. The Taif Agreement of October 1989, formally known as the National Reconciliation Accord, was negotiated in Saudi Arabia to end fifteen years of civil war that had killed an estimated 150,000 people and devastated Beirut. Signed on 22 October and ratified by the Lebanese parliament on 5 November, the accord restructured the country's confessional power-sharing system, shifting executive authority from the Maronite Christian president to the Council of Ministers and rebalancing parliamentary representation between Christian and Muslim communities. Saudi Arabia led the mediation while the United States, Syria, and even Iran worked to shape the outcome behind the scenes — a reminder that Lebanon's fate has long been decided in foreign capitals as much as in Beirut. The agreement mandated Syrian military withdrawal within two years, a provision Syria ignored for sixteen years until 2005. Hezbollah was controversially exempted from militia disarmament on the grounds that it remained a resistance force against Israeli occupation, a carve-out that would define Lebanese politics for the following three decades. The accord ended open warfare but entrenched the same external dependencies and armed factional structures that had made war possible in the first place. Today's ceasefire, reached after Israel's deepest incursion into Lebanese territory since the 1982 and 2006 wars, carries a similar weight: it may freeze the fighting, but the underlying contest between Iran-backed forces, Israeli security imperatives, and Lebanese state sovereignty remains unresolved — as it was when Lebanese parliamentarians gathered in a Saudi city to sign a peace agreement thirty-seven years ago.

### US House Passes War Powers Resolution Challenging Trump on Iran

<!-- audio:top-story-2 -->
The United States House of Representatives voted 215–208 on 4 June to approve a war powers resolution directing President Trump to end military hostilities with Iran — a rare bipartisan rebuke that saw four Republican members cross the aisle to join Democrats after three previous failed attempts.<sup>[1](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMiogFBVV95cUxOaXZBVGtQRWNUQXJnZ1ZIRy1ESnlmMTR6MXZMeGotWEJEV0JwTnJmVXdHYUJZazB3cjhrU2FLN0gyMk0tUlNPZDVYYUpLMzhhTUZFam9aLWpNWjNManFDVzFVTGFudDBCQnJja0V4YXFLNldHRUtvYTZBdVR5eTlZaHFKb1dHSjBHSXNMRHpZR3JGY21Kb1NONEJ3ckoxR3N4d3c?oc=5)</sup> The measure is largely symbolic without Senate ratification, but its passage signals that congressional unease with the administration's approach to Iran policy has grown to a point where the normal partisan discipline on national security no longer holds.<sup>[2](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cj6pldg39deo)</sup> NPR characterised the vote as a direct challenge to executive war-making authority at a moment when US forces remain operationally engaged in the Gulf region.<sup>[3](https://www.npr.org/2026/06/03/nx-s1-5845102/house-iran-war-powers-vote)</sup>

The resolution draws on the 1973 War Powers Resolution — itself passed over President Nixon's veto following Congressional anger at undeclared military operations in Southeast Asia — which requires the president to notify Congress within 48 hours of committing armed forces to hostilities and limits unauthorised deployments to 60 days.<sup>[4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_Powers_Resolution)</sup> That framework has been tested by every administration since, with presidents consistently arguing the resolution is an unconstitutional infringement on commander-in-chief authority. The echoes of the Tanker War of 1984–1988 are also audible: US naval escorts in the Persian Gulf during that conflict drew the country incrementally into the Iran-Iraq confrontation, culminating in the USS Stark incident and a near-war with Tehran, with Congress agitating ineffectually on the sidelines throughout.<sup>[5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanker_war)</sup> Operation Eagle Claw — the disastrous 1980 rescue mission into Iran — similarly exposed the gap between executive military ambition and congressional oversight, and contributed directly to the legislative mood that had produced the War Powers Resolution in the first place.<sup>[6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Eagle_Claw)</sup> The current House vote fits squarely into this long pattern: Congress asserting a principle it lacks the votes or constitutional tools to fully enforce, while the executive branch continues to define the operational reality on the ground.

> **Historical Parallel: The War Powers Resolution — Congressional Pushback on Presidential Military Authority** (1973)
> The 1973 War Powers Resolution, enacted over President Nixon's veto in the aftermath of Vietnam, established the framework that today's House vote invokes. Nixon condemned the law as "unconstitutional and dangerous," and every subsequent president has declined to fully acknowledge its authority. The resolution requires the executive to report military engagements to Congress within 48 hours and withdraw forces within 60 days without explicit authorisation — a clock that administrations have routinely argued never starts. France's 1966 withdrawal from NATO's integrated military command similarly illustrated how domestic political constraints can force a rupture in alliance security arrangements when executive action outpaces democratic legitimacy.<sup>[7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France_and_NATO)</sup> Today's 215–208 vote, while falling far short of a veto-proof majority, represents the most direct congressional challenge to Trump's Iran operations yet recorded, and reflects a growing conviction among some Republicans that the costs of open-ended Gulf engagement now outweigh the political risks of crossing a sitting president.

### Ukraine Records Second Consecutive Month of Net Territorial Gains

<!-- audio:top-story-3 -->
Ukraine pushed Russia back in May 2026 for the second consecutive month, according to analysis from the Institute for the Study of War, with Ukrainian forces recapturing more territory than they lost over the four-week period.<sup>[1](https://www.france24.com/en/ukraine-pushed-russia-back-in-may-for-second-month-running-isw-finds)</sup> Russian forces ceded approximately 93 square miles of Ukrainian territory across the month, a reversal of the grinding advances Moscow had sustained through much of 2025 and early 2026. The shift reflects sustained Ukrainian tactical counterattack operations, complemented by long-range drone and missile strikes reaching deep into Russian logistics and naval infrastructure — including a strike on a warship at the Kronstadt naval base near St. Petersburg and strikes in Crimea that killed at least three people.<sup>[2](https://www.france24.com/en/europe/20260604-ukrainian-strike-kills-three-in-crimea-after-kyiv-targets-st-petersburg-energy-military-sites)</sup>

The territorial reversal coincides with a significant diplomatic development: Presidents Trump and Zelenskyy are reported to have converged on 90–95 percent of a framework peace proposal, suggesting that military momentum and diplomatic progress may for the first time be moving in the same direction simultaneously. The strategic pattern recalls the Korean War experience, where battlefield stalemate and peace talks ran in parallel for over two years before an armistice was signed in 1953 — with both sides using military activity to strengthen their negotiating positions even as diplomats talked.<sup>[3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_War)</sup> The Allied Oil Campaign of 1944–1945 offers another instructive echo: sustained long-range strikes against enemy infrastructure — in that case Axis fuel refineries — proved decisive in degrading operational capacity over time, a logic that Ukrainian commanders are consciously applying to Russian energy and military logistics targets.<sup>[4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_campaign_of_World_War_II)</sup> Whether May's momentum can be sustained through summer will depend on the availability of Western ammunition stocks and whether diplomatic momentum accelerates or stalls the fighting before a formal ceasefire framework is agreed.

> **Historical Parallel: The Korean War Stalemate and Armistice Negotiations** (1951–1953)
> After the initial dramatic advances and retreats of 1950–1951, the Korean War settled into a grinding stalemate along a line close to the original 38th parallel boundary, with peace talks commencing at Kaesong in July 1951 even as fighting continued. For two years, negotiators haggled over prisoner of war repatriation and demarcation lines while commanders launched costly offensives for marginal terrain — each side seeking leverage at the table through battlefield results. The armistice was finally signed at Panmunjom on 27 July 1953, freezing rather than resolving the underlying political conflict. Ukraine's second consecutive month of net gains, even as a peace framework reportedly nears completion, mirrors this dynamic precisely: territorial recovery serves as diplomatic currency, and neither side wishes to accept a ceasefire from a position of perceived weakness.
