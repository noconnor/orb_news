### Russia's Ruling Party Wins Election as Ukraine Strikes Moscow

<!-- audio:top-story-1 -->
Vladimir Putin's United Russia party has secured a commanding parliamentary majority in voting held on 20 and 21 September, even as Ukraine launched what it called the largest aerial bombardment of the Russian capital to date.<sup>[1](https://www.france24.com/en/europe/20260921-live-united-russia-dominates-in-elections-moscow-vows-revenge-after-massive-drone-attack)</sup> The drone assault struck on the final day of voting, causing casualties and damaging infrastructure in and around Moscow, and Russian officials have pledged to intensify strikes on Kyiv and other Ukrainian cities in response.<sup>[2](https://www.france24.com/en/europe/20260920-russia-s-ruling-party-on-track-to-win-election-after-ukraine-s-drone-attacks-on-moscow)</sup> The result cements Kremlin control over the legislature at the same moment the war is escalating into direct strikes on each side's civilian and energy infrastructure, underlining how the conflict now shapes domestic politics on both sides of the front line.

> **Historical Parallel: Wartime Elections Amid Aerial Bombing Campaigns**
> Governments have repeatedly held elections or continued political business while under sustained aerial attack on their own territory. The Blitz and the wider German terror-bombing campaign of 1940–41 targeted British civilian and industrial infrastructure in an attempt to break morale, yet Britain's wartime government continued to function throughout.<sup>[Wikipedia: Strategic bombing during World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_bombing_during_World_War_II)</sup> The Allied oil campaign of 1944–45 later showed how strikes on energy infrastructure specifically could grind down an adversary's capacity to fight without ending the war outright.<sup>[Wikipedia: Oil campaign of World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_campaign_of_World_War_II)</sup> The Winter War of 1939–40 offers a further parallel: a much smaller state absorbed a larger neighbour's offensive while its political institutions kept operating.<sup>[Wikipedia: Winter War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_War)</sup> Like today's exchange of strikes on Moscow and Kyiv, these earlier campaigns show that bombing civilian and infrastructure targets rarely decides a war on its own, but it does harden domestic political consolidation around the incumbent government — precisely what today's United Russia result suggests.

> **Historical Parallel: Gulf Shipping Chokepoints and Oil Shocks**
> Threats to Gulf shipping lanes have repeatedly translated into global economic shocks. The Arab oil embargo of 1973 showed how quickly a Middle East supply disruption could quadruple oil prices and destabilise Western economies.<sup>[Wikipedia: 1973 oil crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_oil_crisis)</sup> The Tanker War of 1984–88, fought during the Iran–Iraq War, saw both sides attack neutral shipping in the Gulf, forcing convoys and reroutes much like today's cautious vessel traffic through Hormuz.<sup>[Wikipedia: Tanker war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanker_war)</sup> The 1990 oil price shock that followed Iraq's invasion of Kuwait, and the Suez Crisis of 1956 before it, both showed how a single chokepoint closure or threat can ripple through global markets within days.<sup>[Wikipedia: 1990 oil price shock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1990_oil_price_shock) · [Wikipedia: Suez Crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Crisis)</sup> Piracy off the coast of Somalia in the 2000s is a more recent reminder that even non-state threats to a shipping lane can force the same costly rerouting seen today.<sup>[Wikipedia: Piracy off the coast of Somalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piracy_off_the_coast_of_Somalia)</sup> Together these episodes suggest the Hormuz slowdown is unlikely to resolve quickly while the underlying Iran–US standoff continues.

### Germany's Centre-Right Suffers Heavy State Election Losses as AfD Surges

<!-- audio:top-story-3 -->
Exit polls from state elections in Berlin and Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania show Chancellor Friedrich Merz's CDU-led bloc taking heavy losses, while the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) posts sharp gains.<sup>[1](https://www.euronews.com/my-europe/2026/09/21/germany-the-left-triumphs-in-berlin-as-afd-surges-in-mecklenburg-western-pomerania)</sup> The Left party has won outright in Berlin, a result that marks a genuine political realignment rather than a routine swing against the governing party. Together the two results point to a squeeze on Germany's traditional centre from both flanks, with voters splitting toward the Left in the capital and toward the AfD in the northeast. The shift carries weight beyond German borders, since German coalition arithmetic and policy direction feed directly into EU-wide decision-making on everything from fiscal rules to migration.

> **Historical Parallel: The Weimar Republic's Political Fragmentation**
> Germany has been here before. The Weimar Republic's constitution used proportional representation, which let smaller and more radical parties win seats rather than forcing voters toward two or three broad blocs, and by the 1919 National Assembly election the political left was already split between the mainstream SPD and the further-left USPD and KPD.<sup>[Wikipedia: Weimar Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weimar_Republic)</sup> Coalition governments during the 1920s repeatedly failed to hold a working majority, and after June 1930 chancellors governed increasingly by presidential decree rather than through parliament. When the Great Depression hit in 1929–30, the Nazi Party's vote share jumped from a marginal force to the largest party in the Reichstag by July 1932, a rise that fed directly on the same fragmentation and coalition paralysis now visible in miniature in today's Berlin and Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania results. The parallel is not that today's Germany faces the same stakes — it plainly does not — but that a splintering centre and gains at both political extremes are a recurring feature of German electoral politics under proportional representation, not a one-off shock.
